title: To Phil
kind: thoughts
date: 2026-09-14
---

It was late October 2017. I was walking between unnaturally planted trees in the middle of an artificial lake full of ducks and algae, reading Walt Whitman’s poems and learning the foundational mathematics behind physics. That was the moment in space and time when I first listened to The Glow, Pt. 2.

Back then, English was far from being my second language—I could barely understand the lyrics without looking them up on the internet—but there was something more profound about that album that I had not yet fully grasped. Almost three years later, I was locked inside my house, basically begging the sky for some calamity to challenge my foundations, due to a pandemic that threatened to take the lives of those I love. And then it happened—I faced death and grief for the first time in my life.

I immediately went back to reading Leaves of Grass, often complementing it with readings of Mark Fisher’s ideas about loneliness, isolation, depression, and music. It was in late 2020 when I returned to feeling alone, there with my headphones on, taking a deep dive into Phil Elverum’s complete discography. And then it came—an epiphany. What felt so familiar about The Glow (and Phil’s voice in general) was its apologetic and tragic prediction about the future: time, and therefore life, is nothing but a wind that keeps blowing against my gravity-degraded face—going away and coming back, with me having no way to predict it.

As I made those connections between ideas, poetry, music, and physics, I was also forging a deep connection to Phil Elverum’s persona and creative mind. I did not quite understand why.

And that brings me to the present day.

Last Thursday, I drove with my father to Tucson, AZ, for a Mount Eerie concert. It took us four hours to get there. Tired from driving through the hot desert, we finally arrived. A strong smell of freshly brewed coffee filled the air. The venue was small and somewhat sketchy. The merchandise table was just there. My father said, “Oh, there is the elephant.” We poured ourselves a cup of coffee while waiting for someone to show up and take my money in exchange for the album. About five minutes later, he emerged from the still-forming crowd, wearing a vibrant green t-shirt and white carpenter Dickies. That person was the human version of the voice in my headphones. A line had already formed, and I was fourth or fifth in turn. I reached the table, took out the two twenty-dollar bills I was willing to exchange for those two polyvinyl chloride-pressed disks contained in a cardboard envelope with an elephant picture on the cover. He looked me in the eye as I handed him the forty dollars. He gave me ten back, our hands subtly touching. With a mellow and humorous voice, he said, “Thank you.” I replied, “No, thank you,” trying to emphasize my gratitude.

And then—another epiphany hit me: we are all humans.
